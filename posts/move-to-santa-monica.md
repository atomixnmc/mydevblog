# From Saigon to Santa Monica: A Silicon Beach Story

The Pacific Ocean was right there. After years of Ho Chi Minh City's chaotic energy—the endless motorbike honking, the humidity that turned every walk into a swimming event, the smell of street food mixing with diesel fumes—I was sitting on Santa Monica Beach watching the sun set over the water, and I wasn't sure what to do with the silence.

Moving to Santa Monica in early 2018 was a reset. SGMedia had closed. Vietnam's tech scene, while growing, didn't have the depth of multiplayer gaming infrastructure I wanted to explore. I had saved enough from the studio's final contracts to survive six months without income. The plan was simple: network, build, and figure out what came next.

Silicon Beach in 2018 was a strange ecosystem. You had genuine gaming talent from Riot, Blizzard, and Naughty Dog mixing with wellness influencers, crypto bros, and people who described themselves as "creative strategists" without irony. The Venice boardwalk was walking distance from offices where engineers were building the next generation of AR experiences.

I landed a consulting gig with a VR startup in Playa Vista within three weeks. The team was building a multiplayer VR experience for enterprise training—think "fire drill simulation but everyone's wearing an Oculus Rift." My Node.js multiplayer experience from SGMedia was directly applicable, and they were paying $150/hour that I would have killed for in Vietnam.

```
Silicon Beach Gamedev Scene (2018):
- Weekly meetups: 3-5 active groups
- Co-working: Cross Campus, WeWork
- Major employers: Riot, Activision, Google
- Indie density: High, especially in VR/AR
```

The cultural shift was jarring. In Vietnam, I was a founder running a studio. In Santa Monica, I was an anonymous contractor. Meetings started with "what's your background" instead of "let's grab pho and talk business." The pace was faster, the expectations higher, and the cost of living was a constant background anxiety.

But the access to talent and ideas was unmatched. I attended talks by engineers who had shipped Call of Duty titles. I collaborated with designers who had built experiences at Disney. The quality of craft in LA's gaming scene pushed me to level up my own standards.

The ocean sunsets never got old. And neither did the realization that I had gone from a borrowed office above a pho shop to the heart of the West Coast tech scene. That journey, in itself, made every struggle worth it.

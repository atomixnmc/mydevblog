# SGMedia: The Founding Story

![](images/2013/sgmedia-founding-story_img-001.png)

The year was 2013. I was sitting in a Hanoi coffee shop with condensation running down the glass and a cracked MacBook Air in front of me. Vietnam's tech scene was still raw—a few co-working spaces, a handful of meetups, and a YouTube gaming ecosystem that was exploding faster than anyone could predict.

I had moved to Ho Chi Minh City six months earlier with a backpack, a laptop, and roughly $3,000 in savings. My plan was vague: build something in games or media, in a country where the cost of living would stretch my runway further than it could in the US or Europe. Vietnam in 2013 was cheap—a bowl of pho cost $1.50, a month in a decent apartment ran $400, and developer salaries were a fraction of Western rates. The tradeoff was infrastructure: frequent power outages, slow internet, and a legal system that made company registration a months-long ordeal.

## The Hanoi Tech Scene in 2013

To understand why I chose Hanoi, you have to understand what the city looked like to a foreign developer in 2013. There was no Techcombank headquarters tower, no major Silicon Valley offices, no venture capital ecosystem to speak of. The startup scene consisted of about 50 people who all knew each other. We met at events hosted by the Hanoi Hub co-working space or at a bar called BiaCraft that served decent IPAs and worse wifi.

The talent pool was the real draw. Vietnam had (and still has) a young, technically educated population with a strong work ethic. Universities were churning out CS graduates, but the local industry couldn't absorb them all at competitive wages. A skilled Unity developer in Hanoi in 2013 cost about $800-1,200/month, compared to $6,000+ in San Francisco. The arbitrage was real, but it came with caveats: English fluency varied, experience with modern game development pipelines was rare, and mentorship was entirely on me.

The YouTube gaming ecosystem in Vietnam was a different beast entirely. Vietnamese gamers were voracious consumers of gaming content on YouTube, but almost all the content was in English or Chinese. There was a massive gap for Vietnamese-language gaming content. Channels that started filling that gap in 2012-2013 grew to hundreds of thousands of subscribers within two years. I saw this opportunity and decided that SGMedia would sit at the intersection of game development and YouTube content.

## The First Hire

My first employee was a Unity developer named Minh, whom I found through a forum post on a Vietnamese game development board. He was 22, fresh out of university, and spoke English well enough that we could communicate about code. His portfolio consisted of two half-finished mobile games and a lot of enthusiasm.

I offered him $700/month and a profit-sharing arrangement that would ultimately pay him nothing. He accepted. That first hire taught me something crucial about building in emerging markets: enthusiasm and raw talent are abundant, but structured experience is scarce. Minh was a good programmer by any measure, but he had never worked on a team, never used version control collaboratively, and never shipped to a live audience. I had to be both the technical lead and the teacher.

We set up desks in a shared apartment in District 2. The rent was $500 for a two-bedroom that served as office, kitchen, and occasional crash pad. We worked on client Unity projects during the day and built our own prototypes at night. The lines between work and life blurred completely, which felt romantic at 26 and feels unsustainable at 40.

## The YouTube Bet

While we built games, I also started a YouTube channel focused on Unity development tutorials—but in Vietnamese. The idea was to capture the growing Vietnamese gaming audience while establishing SGMedia as a technical authority in the local market.

The first video was a 15-minute tutorial on Unity's animation system. I recorded it with a $20 microphone and screen recording software. The audio quality was terrible. The content was solid. It got about 200 views in the first week. By the end of the first year, the channel had 5,000 subscribers and was generating about $200/month in ad revenue.

The YouTube strategy worked better than I expected for three reasons. First, there was genuine demand: Vietnamese developers wanted to learn game development but most tutorials were in English. Second, the algorithm rewarded consistency—I published every Tuesday and Thursday without fail. Third, the channel became a recruiting pipeline. Several later hires found us through the tutorials and joined because they trusted the technical quality of the content.

## The Client Work Engine

By mid-2014, we had a small but steady stream of client work. International studios would hire us for Unity development at rates that were cheap for them and life-changing for us. A $15,000 contract from a Singapore studio paid for four months of operations. A $30,000 project from a Japanese mobile publisher covered the entire year.

The pattern was always the same: a studio with more work than capacity would offload the less critical pieces to SGMedia. We built mini-games, implemented UI systems, and created art assets. The work was never glamorous, but it paid the bills and sharpened our technical skills. We got really good at Unity's 2D tooling, particle systems, and shader graph, because that's what the clients needed.

I made a deliberate choice to take client work even when it conflicted with our original IP ambitions. The reasoning was simple: our original games might fail, but client work taught us process, discipline, and how to ship on a deadline. Those skills would transfer whether we succeeded or failed with our own projects. In hindsight, this was the right call—the client work ultimately sustained us for years longer than we would have lasted on original IP alone.

## Building the Studio Culture

SGMedia's culture was a product of its constraints. We couldn't afford fancy office perks, so we built culture around what we had: shared meals, late-night coding sessions, and a collective obsession with making things.

Every Friday afternoon, we'd stop working at 4 PM and play whatever game we were most into that week. These sessions were part team building, part competitive analysis. We'd dissect mechanics, discuss UX patterns, and argue about what made games fun. Those arguments taught me more about game design than any book.

We also established a tradition of "ship Fridays"—if a feature was close to done, we'd stay late together and push it across the finish line. The ritual of deploying together, watching the build succeed, and then going out for bia hơi (fresh beer, $0.25 a glass) created bonds that outlasted the company itself.

## The Projects That Defined Us

A few projects stand out from the SGMedia years:

**Hanoi Showdown** was our first game jam entry, built for the Global Game Jam 2014. It was a local multiplayer fighting game set in a Hanoi street market. Two players, one keyboard, absurd characters. It won nothing, but it proved we could ship a complete game in 48 hours. The code was a disaster; the experience was invaluable.

**The Logistics Simulator** was our biggest client project: a Unity-based training application for a logistics company. It simulated warehouse operations, complete with forklift physics and inventory management. It wasn't a game, but it taught us about real-time system design, state management, and client communication. The project paid for six months of operations.

**Mekong Delta** was the dream project. An open-world fishing and exploration game set in Vietnam's Mekong Delta region. We'd been prototyping it on and off for two years, building procedural river generation, dynamic weather, and a fishing mechanic with realistic physics. The game was 60% complete when the studio ran out of money. It's still the project I'm most proud of, even unfinished.

## The Hanoi Advantage

Living and working in Hanoi gave SGMedia a perspective that Western studios didn't have. We understood the Southeast Asian gaming market intimately—the mobile-first adoption, the preference for free-to-play, the social gaming culture that revolved around Facebook and Zalo rather than Steam.

This local knowledge was a double-edged sword. It made us valuable to international clients who wanted to reach the Vietnamese market, but it also meant we were building for an audience with less purchasing power. A Vietnamese mobile gamer might spend $5/month on in-app purchases, compared to $50 for a US player. We had to build for volume or accept lower per-user revenue.

The studio also benefited from Vietnam's manufacturing culture. The phrase "làm được" (it can be done) is deeply ingrained in the Vietnamese work ethic. Resources were scarce, but creativity in the face of constraints was abundant. We built things with rigged hardware, pirated software licenses (I'm not proud of this, but it's honest), and sheer stubbornness.

## What I'd Tell My 2013 Self

If I could go back to that Hanoi coffee shop and talk to myself before signing the incorporation papers, here's what I'd say:

First, don't scale the team before you've validated the revenue. Hire slowly, fire quickly, and keep your burn rate at a level that one lost client won't kill you. We hired person number four before we had consistent revenue from person four's salary. That's not ambition; that's gambling.

Second, the YouTube channel was the right bet, but you should have gone harder on it. The tutorials were good, but they should have been structured into a paid course. Educational content has a much higher ceiling than ad-supported video, and we never exploited that.

Third, build your own IP in parallel with client work from day one. Don't wait for the "right time" to start your own game. The right time never comes. Even 20% time on original projects would have resulted in at least one shipped title by year three, which would have been more than we shipped in the entire five-year run.

Fourth, learn to say no. We took client projects that didn't align with our skills, that paid poorly, and that drained our creative energy. A "yes" to the wrong project is a "no" to the right one.

## The Legacy

SGMedia didn't become the Southeast Asian game development powerhouse I imagined. But it did prove something: a small team in an emerging market, with limited resources and limitless ambition, could build professional-quality games and sustain itself for five years. That's not a failure. That's a five-year run of making things, learning constantly, and building a community.

The YouTube channel is still online, a time capsule of a particular era in Vietnamese game development. The open-source tools we released still get occasional GitHub stars. The former team members are scattered across the industry—some at major studios, some at tech companies, one who started their own studio.

And somewhere in a closet in Ho Chi Minh City, there's a backup drive with Mekong Delta's source code, waiting for the improbable sequel.

## The Technical Stack We Built On

SGMedia's technical decisions shaped our output in ways I only fully appreciate in retrospect. We bet on Unity when it was still finding its footing in 2D development. We chose the open-source route for our tools whenever possible—Blender for 3D art, GIMP for textures, Audacity for audio. The budget didn't allow for Photoshop licenses or Maya subscriptions, and the constraint forced us to learn our tools deeply.

Our CI/CD pipeline was a shared Google Drive folder. Deployments were manual. Version control was Git with a branching strategy that one ex-Googler friend described as "aggressively improvised." We had no automated tests for the first two years. The first time I set up a proper deployment pipeline felt like magic, and I remember thinking, "We should have done this years ago."

The YouTube channel ran on a different tech stack entirely. We used OBS for recording, Audacity for voiceover, and DaVinci Resolve for editing. The bottleneck was never the tools; it was the consistency. Publishing twice a week for five years created a library of 400+ videos. The oldest ones are painful to watch—bad audio, awkward pacing, terrible lighting. But they document the journey from beginner to competent, and that authenticity resonated with viewers.

## The Pivot That Almost Worked

In 2016, I attempted a strategic pivot that I still think about. The idea: transform SGMedia from a game development studio into a game development education platform. The YouTube channel was growing, the audience was engaged, and there was clear demand for structured Vietnamese-language game dev courses.

We launched a pilot course—"Unity 2D Game Development from Scratch"—priced at $50 for 20 hours of video content plus live Q&A sessions. We sold 47 seats in the first month, generating $2,350 in revenue. The economics were better than client work: higher margin, less custom work, and the content had long-tail value.

The plan was to produce four courses per year, building a catalog that would generate passive revenue while we continued client work and original IP development. It was the right idea, but I executed it alongside everything else instead of replacing something with it. We didn't have the bandwidth to maintain the course quality while also running game development projects, and the course updates fell behind as Unity released new versions.

The pivot taught me a lesson I've applied ever since: a pivot isn't an addition, it's a replacement. You have to stop doing the old thing to make room for the new thing. Partial pivots create more work, not less.

## The Financial Mechanics of a Saigon Studio

The cost structure of a Vietnamese game studio in 2013-2018 deserves its own analysis because it's fundamentally different from a Western studio's economics.

Our biggest expense was always salaries, but the nature of that expense changed over time. In 2013, a mid-level Unity developer cost about $800/month. By 2018, the same developer could command $2,000/month, because the tech industry in Vietnam had exploded. The salary arbitrage that made our business model viable in 2013 had eroded by 50% in five years.

Meanwhile, our revenue was in US dollars (from international clients) but our expenses were increasingly in Vietnamese dong. The exchange rate fluctuated but generally favored the dong strengthening against the dollar. This squeezed margins from both directions: costs rose in dollar terms, and the dollar bought less locally.

The lesson for anyone building in an emerging market: your cost advantage is time-limited. The market will mature, salaries will rise, and the arbitrage will shrink. You need to use that window to build something defensible—a brand, an audience, a proprietary technology—that doesn't depend on cheap labor.

## The Saigon That Was

Saigon in 2013 was a different city. District 2 was mostly empty fields and half-built apartment blocks. The metro didn't exist. Coffee shops that are now international chains were local businesses. The tech scene fit in one room at the Saigon Hub co-working space.

The city's transformation mirrors the arc of the company. We both started raw and ambitious, grew fast, hit hard constraints, and emerged changed on the other side. Saigon is now a serious tech hub with international investment, global company offices, and a startup ecosystem that produces genuine unicorns. SGMedia didn't become a unicorn, but we were part of the foundation that made the ecosystem possible.

Every successful startup in Vietnam today stands on the graveyards of the ones that came before. The failures are invisible; only the successes get press. But the failures taught the lessons that the successes applied. SGMedia was one of those failures, and I'm okay with that.

The drive in my closet with Mekong Delta's source code isn't a relic of failure. It's evidence that we tried, we built, and we shipped. And in the end, that's all any of us can do.

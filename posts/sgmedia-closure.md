# Closing SGMedia: The Day I Merged the Final Branch

![](images/2018/sgmedia-closure_img-001.png)

Shutting down a company is like deleting a git branch. You've put weeks of work into it. It had a purpose. There were merge conflicts you fought through, late-night commits, features that worked and features that didn't. And then one day you realize the branch has diverged too far from master, and it's time to let it go.

I signed the dissolution papers for SGMedia on a humid Thursday afternoon in October 2018. The receptionist at the law firm asked what kind of media company it was. "Game development," I said. She smiled and said her nephew wanted to make games. I didn't have the heart to tell her that statistically, her nephew would fail too.

## Why We Closed: The Honest Answer

The short answer is money. The long answer is more nuanced and probably more useful to someone reading this who's considering starting a game studio in a market like Vietnam.

SGMedia was profitable for exactly two months of its five-year run. In February 2017, we hit $14,000 in revenue and $11,000 in expenses. I framed that P&L statement. It's probably still in a box somewhere. Profitability is rare in indie game development—most studios exist in a perpetual state of "next project will be the one."

We sustained ourselves through a combination of client work (Unity development for international studios), ad revenue from YouTube content, and a couple of mobile game launches that never quite hit. The client work paid the bills but consumed the creative energy needed for original IP. The YouTube ad revenue was volatile—a single algorithm change could drop CPMs by 60% overnight. The mobile games generated maybe $3,000 total across their lifetimes.

The math never worked at the scale we needed. Hanoi developer salaries were rising fast as the tech boom hit Vietnam. We were competing with outsourcing firms that charged $30/hour and startups that offered equity. We offered a mission—build cool games—but missions don't pay rent.

## What We Actually Built

It's easy to look back and see only the failures, so let me be deliberate about listing what we shipped. Because we did ship things.

**Game Jams and Prototypes.** We participated in seven game jams over five years and placed in the top 10 for three of them. One of our jam games, a two-player dueling game called "Hanoi Showdown," got featured on a indie gaming blog and had about 20,000 downloads. It was built in 48 hours on a caffeine bender.

**Client Work.** We built Unity-based training simulations for a logistics company, a 2D platformer for an educational publisher, and contributed art assets to a mid-core mobile RPG that actually charted in Southeast Asia for a month. This work paid the bills but none of it had our name on it.

**Original IP.** Our flagship project was "Mekong Delta," a 3D open-world fishing and exploration game set in Vietnam's river country. We had a playable prototype with procedurally generated waterways, dynamic weather, and a fishing mechanic that I was genuinely proud of. The project was about 60% complete when we ran out of runway.

The prototype still exists on a hard drive in my closet. Sometimes I think about open-sourcing it.

## The Team That Made It Work

At our peak, SGMedia had seven people: two developers, two artists, one designer, one community manager, and me doing everything else—business development, project management, QA, and occasionally writing shaders at 2 AM.

The team was the best part of the studio. They were talented, passionate, and working for below-market salaries because they believed in what we were building. I learned more about management from those five years than from any book or course. The most important lesson: transparency is not weakness. When I started sharing the financial reality with the team openly—not just the good months but the bad ones too—we started making better collective decisions.

We lost two key people in 2018, both to larger tech companies offering 3x their current salary. I couldn't blame them. I wrote their reference letters personally and meant every word.

## The Financial Autopsy

Let me show you the actual numbers from our final year, because I wish someone had shown me this math before I started:

| Category | Monthly Avg | Annual Total |
|----------|-------------|--------------|
| Rent & utilities | $1,200 | $14,400 |
| Salaries (5 people avg) | $8,500 | $102,000 |
| Contractors & freelancers | $1,800 | $21,600 |
| Software licenses & tools | $400 | $4,800 |
| Marketing & ads | $600 | $7,200 |
| Hardware & equipment | $300 | $3,600 |
| Legal & accounting | $200 | $2,400 |
| **Total expenses** | **$13,000** | **$156,000** |

| Revenue streams | Monthly Avg | Annual Total |
|-----------------|-------------|--------------|
| Client development | $7,000 | $84,000 |
| YouTube ad revenue | $2,500 | $30,000 |
| Mobile game sales | $250 | $3,000 |
| **Total revenue** | **$9,750** | **$117,000** |

We were bleeding about $3,250 per month. The burn rate was covered by my personal savings and occasional consulting work. I had told myself this was "investing in the business." In retrospect, it was denial with a spreadsheet.

The fatal mistake was obvious in hindsight: we never had a clear revenue model for our original IP. We assumed "build it and they will come." The gaming market doesn't work that way. Discovery is the hardest problem, not development. You can build the best fishing game ever made, but if nobody hears about it, it doesn't matter.

## The Closing Process

Closing a company in Vietnam is bureaucratically complex. You don't just stop paying rent and walk away. There are tax clearances to obtain, social insurance to settle, and government forms to file in triplicate. The process took three months longer than I expected and cost about $2,000 in legal fees.

The hardest part was telling the team. I called everyone individually over the course of a week. Some cried. Some said they'd expected it. One person thanked me for the experience. I wrote severance checks that I couldn't really afford from my personal account. It felt like the right thing to do.

We shipped the final build of Mekong Delta as a free download on itch.io. About 4,000 people downloaded it. The comments were kind—"beautiful world," "love the atmosphere," "wish it was finished." I read every single one.

## What I'd Do Differently

With the clarity of hindsight, here's what I'd change:

**Validate revenue before team size.** I scaled the team to seven people before we had proven any revenue stream could sustain us. I should have kept the core team at three until we had a game generating at least $5,000/month in recurring revenue.

**Ship faster and smaller.** Our ambition exceeded our runway on every project. Mekong Delta was too big for a five-person studio. We should have shipped three smaller games instead of one 60%-complete large one.

**Build the audience before the game.** We started marketing Mekong Delta about three months before our planned launch. We should have been building a community from day one of development. The games that succeed on Steam are the ones with wishlists before they have gameplay footage.

**Price for sustainability, not passion.** I undercharged for client work because I wanted to be the "friendly" studio. Friendly doesn't pay the bills. I should have charged market rate or higher and let the work speak for itself.

## The Aftermath

After the studio closed, I took a consulting contract with a Singapore-based tech company. The money was good. The work was fine. I felt hollow for about six months.

The post-studio period taught me something important: the identity you build around your company becomes a trap. I was "SGMedia founder" for so long that I forgot who I was without it. The grief wasn't about the money or the failed game. It was about losing the story I told myself about who I was.

I eventually started building HyperGraph, partly because I missed building things and partly because I needed a project that was mine. The lessons from SGMedia—about running lean, about validating before building, about separating identity from output—infiltrated everything I did differently this time.

## What Remains

The SGMedia website is down. The GitHub organization is archived. The YouTube channel still exists, a time capsule of 2016-era Unity tutorials and behind-the-scenes dev logs.

A few things survived. The code for some internal tools that I open-sourced and people actually use. Relationships with former team members who've gone on to do amazing things at bigger studios. The muscle memory of shipping under pressure.

And the hard drive in my closet, with Mekong Delta's source code, waiting for someone to fork it.

![](images/2018/sgmedia-closure_img-003.png)

I don't regret starting SGMedia. I regret some of the decisions, but not the act of trying. The studio taught me things about building software, managing people, and knowing when to walk away that I couldn't have learned any other way. The branch is deleted, but the commits still exist in the history.

## Lessons That Transferred to My Next Venture

The closure of SGMedia wasn't the end of my entrepreneurial journey—it was the tuition for my next one. When I started HyperGraph, I carried forward specific operational patterns that came directly from the SGMedia experience.

**Revenue-first development.** HyperGraph's first version was a minimal API that I sold to exactly one customer before writing a line of infrastructure code. The customer paid $500/month for a prototype that existed as a single Python file running on a $5 VPS. That $500 validated the concept before I spent a dollar on cloud infrastructure. SGMedia spent years building before validating; HyperGraph validated before building.

**Fixed-cost cap.** HyperGraph's burn rate was set at $2,000/month from day one—my personal consulting income minus living expenses. When the burn hit that cap, I stopped spending. No credit card debt, no dipping into savings, no fantasies about "next month will be better." This discipline came directly from watching SGMedia's burn rate grow unchecked.

**Single-threaded focus.** SGMedia tried to do everything: client work, original IP, YouTube content, community building. HyperGraph does exactly one thing: build an AI-native knowledge graph. Every feature proposal gets measured against that mission. If it doesn't serve the knowledge graph, it doesn't get built, no matter how interesting it is.

**Team size as a liability.** HyperGraph was solo for the first 18 months. No hires, no contractors, no co-founders. When I finally brought someone on, it was for a specific, time-bounded contract role. The cost of coordination—meetings, context transfer, management overhead—is exponential in team size, not linear. SGMedia had seven people and a coordination nightmare. HyperGraph had one person and no meetings.

## The Emotional Arc of Closing

There's a specific emotional sequence to shutting down something you built, and I think it's worth describing because nobody talks about it.

**Stage 1: Denial.** I spent three months convincing myself that the next client project would save us. The next YouTube video would go viral. The next game would be the hit. I re-ran the financial projections with optimistic assumptions and pretended the math worked.

**Stage 2: Bargaining.** I offered to take a salary cut to zero. I offered to convert debt to equity. I reached out to investors I had previously dismissed. All of these were attempts to avoid the final decision—to keep the company alive on life support.

**Stage 3: The decision.** It came on a Tuesday morning, not in a dramatic moment. I opened the bank account, saw the balance, and realized I was making decisions based on fear rather than opportunity. The company wasn't serving me or the team anymore. We were serving the company. That realization made the decision clear.

**Stage 4: Execution mode.** The three months of winding down were the most productive period of the company's existence. We shipped the final build. We paid off debts. We filed paperwork. The clarity of an ending is powerful—you stop optimizing for an uncertain future and start finishing things properly.

**Stage 5: The void.** After the last form was filed, there was silence. No Slack notifications. No meetings. No "we need to talk about the server bill." The silence was the hardest part. I had defined myself by SGMedia for five years, and suddenly that definition was gone.

It took about six months to rebuild a sense of identity that wasn't tied to a company. The process was uncomfortable but necessary. I wrote code for fun again—not for a client, not for a product, just for the joy of it. That was the first sign that I was healing.

## What I'd Tell Other Founders in the Same Position

If you're reading this while contemplating closing your own studio or startup, here's what I wish someone had told me:

- The sunk cost fallacy is strongest when you're closest to the decision. You've invested years, money, identity. None of that changes whether the future math works. Judge the future on future terms.
- Your team will handle the news better than you expect. They've seen the signs. They've done the math. What they fear is uncertainty, not the closure itself. Give them clarity and they'll handle the rest.
- The paperwork will take longer than you think. Start it now.
- You will feel relief, not just grief. The weight of keeping a struggling company alive is enormous. When you finally put it down, you'll wonder how you carried it so long.
- The next thing you build will be better. Not because you're smarter, but because you've paid for experience with real losses. That tuition is expensive, but the education is permanent.

## Code as Legacy

I archived the SGMedia GitHub organization a few months after closing. Before I did, I extracted the useful open-source components into separate repositories:

- A Unity animation utility library that we used across multiple projects (MIT license, still maintained by contributors).
- A simple ECS (Entity-Component-System) framework we built for Mekong Delta's NPC AI.
- A collection of JME3 shaders from our prototype work.

These small contributions to the open-source community feel more meaningful than the company itself. Code outlives companies. The Unity animation library has 200+ GitHub stars and gets downloaded a few thousand times per month. It's not much, but it means that some piece of SGMedia is still running somewhere, helping someone build something.

That, more than the company registration or the logo or the bank account, is what I wanted when I started: to build things that outlast me. SGMedia as a legal entity is gone. But the code, the relationships, and the lessons are still here.

# Closing SGMedia: The Day I Merged the Final Branch

Shutting down a company is like deleting a git branch. You've put weeks of work into it. It had a purpose. There were merge conflicts you fought through, late-night commits, features that worked and features that didn't. And then one day you realize the branch has diverged too far from master, and it's time to let it go.

The decision to close SGMedia came in late 2017. We had survived the YouTube crash, pivoted to interactive media, and built a solid reputation. But the numbers told a story I couldn't ignore: revenue was flat, costs were rising, and the team was exhausted.

```
Final Financial Snapshot (2017):
Revenue: $180,000 (flat vs 2016)
Costs: $165,000 (up 12%)
Net: $15,000
Founder salary: $0 (last 4 months)
```

I spent three months winding things down. Existing contracts got completed—I wasn't going to leave clients hanging. The team received severance packages that ate up most of that $15,000 net. Hardware was sold to employees at a discount. The office lease was the hardest to break—three months penalty on a 12-month sublease.

The IP distribution was surprisingly emotional. We had built a multiplayer framework that I genuinely believed in. I pushed it to a public repo under the [atomixgame](https://github.com/atomixgame) org where it sat unmaintained but available. Automated build scripts, internal tools, and documentation went to archive repos. Some of it is still used by developers who occasionally email me with questions about a decade-old codebase.

What survived? The knowledge. The architecture patterns for Node.js multiplayer. The WebGL deployment tricks that I still reference. The understanding of how to run a team on a budget that would make Silicon Valley laugh. And the [sgmhn.com](https://sgmhn.com) domain, which I keep alive because it feels wrong to let the last artifact die.

The git branch was merged into master, tagged `v1.0-closed`, and pushed one final time. Then I walked away.

Every founder should know when to stop fighting for something that's already over. SGMedia had a good run. It funded experiments, paid talented people, and taught me lessons that five years of corporate work never could. That's not failure. That's a completed project.

# The YouTube Gaming Bubble: A Postmortem

I watched my YouTube revenue drop 70% in three months, and there was absolutely nothing I could do about it.

From late 2013 through most of 2015, YouTube gaming was a gold rush. Let's Play channels were pulling millions of views. Gaming was the second-largest category on the platform after music. Ad rates were healthy, CPMs hovered around $5-8 for gaming content, and the algorithm loved anything with "gameplay" in the title.

SGMedia ran a network of gaming channels that I'm almost embarrassed to name now. Think "Epic Gaming Moments #42" levels of content. But it worked. We hit 50 million total views across the network, and the ad revenue funded the studio's experiments. It felt sustainable.

It wasn't.

The first sign was the algorithm update in early 2015. Watch time became the dominant ranking signal, and our short-form highlight clips tanked. We pivoted to longer content—15-minute edited episodes with commentary. Revenue recovered partially, but production costs tripled.

Then came the adpocalypse of 2016. Major advertisers pulled spend after content appeared next to extremist videos. YouTube responded by demonetizing anything remotely risky, and their automated system couldn't distinguish between a violent game and actual violence. Gaming channels got caught in the crossfire.

Our CPM dropped from $6 to $1.80 almost overnight.

```
Revenue Timeline:
Q3 2014: $7,200/month (peak, 12 channels)
Q1 2015: $5,400/month (algorithm change)
Q3 2015: $8,100/month (pivot to longer content)
Q1 2016: $2,100/month (first adpocalypse)
Q3 2016: $1,100/month (recovery never came)
```

I made the call to wind down the content division in late 2016. We kept a single channel running for another year, but I had lost faith in the platform. Building a business on someone else's algorithm is like building a house on a floodplain. It looks great until the water rises.

The honest truth? We should have diversified revenue streams the moment we hit $5,000/month. Sponsorships, merchandise, Patreon—any of these would have created a buffer. Instead, we went all-in on AdSense and paid the price.

I don't regret it. That YouTube money funded the multiplayer experiments and the Unity R&D that defined SGMedia's best work. But the next time someone tells me "just start a YouTube channel," I laugh.
